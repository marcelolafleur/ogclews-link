\section{Policy and Economic Feedback}
\label{sec:illustration}

\subsection{Carbon pricing}

A carbon price changes the relative cost of technologies in CLEWS. Where the policy
raises revenue, its use also matters: household transfers have different effects
from public spending or debt reduction. The link distinguishes production costs
from payments to government, so a charge is counted once and matched to its receipts.

Figure~\ref{fig:carbon} shows a household-energy tax based on US\$50 per tonne of
CO$_2$, with revenue returned through transfers. Consumption falls
initially, then recovers, while output remains below the control. This is the response
to the tax and transfer policy together, not an estimate of recycling alone or of
an economy-wide carbon tax.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{channel-macro-carbon.pdf}
\caption{Carbon pricing: the economy's response to the standalone household-energy
tax and transfer policy. This policy experiment is separate from the combined case
below; it does not identify the effect of transfers without a matched tax-only comparison.}
\label{fig:carbon}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Private investment incentives}

A tax credit, accelerated depreciation or a business-tax change alters the cost of
investing in generation. Firms respond through investment and production, while
government bears the fiscal cost. A credit shifts part of the investor's cost to
government without changing the cost of the physical asset
(Table~\ref{tab:private-incentive}).

This is a policy incentive, not a change in technology. It can accompany the
capital-intensity channel when the technology change and the support to investors
are specified separately.

\smallskip
\raggedright The credit changes who pays; it does not make the physical asset cost

\subsection{The planning discount rate}

The discount rate changes the balance between paying now and paying later.
A lower rate gives greater weight to future operating savings and can favour
long-lived assets with higher initial costs. Table~\ref{tab:discount} shows the
arithmetic behind that choice.

In the combined example, the link records an 8.16\% rate, obtained by averaging
the model's household portfolio return over the first ten years. That return can
inform CLEWS, but is not automatically the appropriate public-planning rate.
Risk, taxes and the commercial or social perspective must be reconciled. No
resource-system response to this rate was solved in the example.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering\small
\caption{Discounting: the present value of the same cost at 4\% and at the rate
recorded by the link. The 4\% comparison is illustrative, not a baseline estimate;
neither column is a solved CLEWS investment response.}
\label{tab:discount}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L rr@{}}
\toprule
Cost and payment date & 4\% comparison & 8.16\% recorded rate \\
\midrule
100 paid now & 100.0 & 100.0 \\
100 paid in 20 years & 45.6 & 20.8 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\smallskip
\raggedright At the lower rate, avoiding a future operating cost is worth more today.
The example uses a single future payment; a resource plan discounts its full cost path.
\end{table}

\subsection{Demand}

Households and industries respond to resource choices in ways that change the
plan itself. Higher electricity costs can reduce consumption or production;
investment and health improvements can increase activity. The return link uses
those responses to revise the requirements CLEWS must meet.

In the example, the link uses the change in electricity-sector output as a proxy
for the change in demand. Table~\ref{tab:demand-return} shows the resulting
instruction: multiply each year's baseline requirement by the model's ratio.
For 2030, the ratio is about 0.9355, implying a 6.45\% reduction. The resource
model would then reassess supply and investment. These signals were calculated,
but the example did not complete that return solve.
