\section{Implications for Policy}
\label{sec:implications}

The Philippine example is illustrative, but it highlights three questions that a
resource assessment should address.

First, where do the costs of a plan fall? In the example, most of the economy-wide effect
of higher electricity costs comes through industry rather than household bills. Measures
that protect household bills address only part of the cost; the effects on production,
prices and wages carry the rest. At the same time, households differ in how much of their
budget goes to electricity and in how they are affected by changes in wages, returns and
taxes. The distribution of costs across ages and incomes, and the design of any transfers,
should be part of the assessment rather than an afterthought.

Second, how is investment financed and valued? Public infrastructure financed by debt can
displace private investment, and investment incentives shift costs to the public budget. The
discount rate used to compare present and future costs is itself a policy choice: at the
return the economy requires on capital, future savings count for much less than at a lower
planning rate, and the choice changes which technologies look cheapest.

Third, how do the plan and the economy respond to each other? Cleaner air saves lives and
reduces illness, benefits that should be valued directly rather than through their effect on
GDP. Higher prices reduce the demand for electricity, so a plan built for the original demand
can overestimate the capacity the economy will need.

The choice of a resource plan therefore concerns more than the least-cost technology mix. It
also concerns the timing of investment, its financing, and the distribution of costs and
benefits across households and generations. OG--CLEWS brings these questions into the
resource assessment.
